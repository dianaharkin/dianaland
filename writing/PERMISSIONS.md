## The rule

The engine does not decide what is publishable. Diana does, out loud, and it gets
written down here. An empty table means everything is held — which is the correct
state before that conversation happens, not a failure.

## How to fill this in

Not by handing her a list of topics and asking her to tick boxes. That asks her to
imagine every future piece at once, and people answer that question badly.

Do it per piece, in the moment, when there's an actual draft in front of her:
*"this one draws on the page about ___ - are you good with that being public?"*
Write down what she says, in her words, here. Over time the table fills itself and
the engine needs to ask less.

Some answers will be "yes, but not yet". Some will be "yes, if you don't name him".
Those are the useful ones. Record them exactly, including the condition.

## The three that are held hardest

These are named here so this file is self-contained. The engine gates on them by
default, every time, and they do not become publishable by being mentioned often:

- **Her sobriety**
- **Her father**
- **Her body**, when it is the subject rather than the weather — "sore but strong" on
  the way to a rehearsal is not this; a page about how she feels about her body is.

Being held does not mean unusable. The engine still trains on all of it, and it will
still sometimes lift **one line** off a private page and offer her that line on its
own. That offer is a question, not a decision.

## The exact thing to say

Not a topic list, not a form. One sentence, in the moment, with a real draft in front
of her:

> "This one comes off the page about ___ - I don't have a yes from you on that.
> Want it?"

Then stop and wait. Do not draft ahead of the answer, and do not soften the question by
guessing the answer inside it ("I assume you'd be fine with…").

If she says no, say "got it" and move on. Never ask twice about the same thing in the
same conversation, and never come back to a no in order to test whether it has softened.

## What a good row looks like

Record her words, not a summary of them, and keep every condition attached. These are
the shapes that actually come up — they are examples of **recording**, not answers she
has given:

| Topic or entry | What she said | Date | Who asked |
|---|---|---|---|
| *example — the Catskills flower crown photo* | *"yeah that one's fine, everyone's seen it"* | | |
| *example — the page about quitting the band* | *"yes but not while I'm still playing with them"* | | |
| *example — the argument in the kitchen* | *"fine if you don't name him"* | | |

**The conditional ones are the valuable ones.** "Yes, but not yet" and "yes, if you
don't name him" carry more than a bare yes, and losing the condition is how a yes turns
into a betrayal six months later. Copy the condition down word for word.

A **"yes"** is not a standing yes for the topic. It is a yes for that piece, unless she
says otherwise in so many words. When she does give a standing yes, write the word
**standing** in the row so nobody has to infer it.

She can also **take a yes back.** If she does, strike the row through rather than
deleting it, and put the date. The record of what she once agreed to matters as much as
what she agrees to now.

## What does not go in this table

Anything she hasn't been asked. Leave the row out rather than guessing at it. An empty
table is the honest state before the conversation; a table full of assumed answers is
worse than no table at all.
